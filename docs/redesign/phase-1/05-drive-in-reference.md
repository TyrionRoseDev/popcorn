# Drive-in reference: what the artefacts were and what they did

This file records what twelve real drive-in artefacts looked like and what job each one did, so Phase 2 can borrow function and not retro styling. It uses patents, National Register nominations, museum records, operators' own pages and 1916 to 1966 trade papers on Lantern. It uses no retro design references. The scope is mostly US drive-ins from 1933 to the 1960s, with later notes on radio sound. Each section gives the real function, the visual vocabulary, a raw diary mapping and the sources.

Three limits apply to the evidence. Lantern pages are OCR scans, and I read them and the patents through a fetch tool that summarises, so check any quotation against the scan before reusing it. I read the National Register forms directly as page images. Trade adverts are sellers' claims, and I mark them as adverts.

Sourcing is thin for these topics:

- Typefaces. No source I read names a letterform for marquee letters or neon signs.
- Wear and ageing. Almost nothing describes it, apart from storm damage.
- Roll tickets as a physical object. I found tickets, stubs and packs, but no source that states the roll format for drive-ins.
- The FM tuning sign. The change to radio is well dated, but I found no description of the physical sign.
- Printed patron notices. I found the scripts of screen trailers and later web rules, but no surviving printed windshield notice.
- Menu boards. One sentence in one source.

Diary mappings are raw Phase 2 ideas. None of them is a decision.

## 1. Marquee letter boards and changeable letters

**Real function.** The board told passing drivers what was on tonight, and it also had to sell the idea of going to a drive-in at all, according to Theatre Catalog 1955-56. It had to read from the road, and Adler adverts stress reading "from an angle or at a distance". Copy changed with the programme, so speed of change was a selling point. A 1962 Wagner advert sells a "Mechanical Hand" that changes the message in half the time needed by two men on ladders. The Beverly Drive-In's board had a divided letter board for two feature titles, which fits the double bill. At the 99W the sign's base was a small storage room for the letters.

**Visual vocabulary.** Letters slipped onto guide bars in front of lit glass panels, and slots or studs held them in place, per Showmen's Trade Review 1946. Wagner advertised patented tapered-slot letters that "won't slip out of place" in 1962. Wagner's 1946 plastic letters were translucent, in red, green, blue, amber and opaque black, in 4, 10 and 17 inch sizes, with the colour running all the way through. Adler sold opaque cast aluminium letters with bevelled edges and a recessed face, in sizes listed as 4 to 24 inches, and its 1937 advert describes "broad strokes". Real boards were large. The El Monte board was 30 feet wide with 17 and 10 inch translucent red and blue letters. The Sunset in Factoria, Washington, had a board 20 feet long and four lines high with 12 inch cast aluminium letters, wood supports and gooseneck reflectors. The Caro in Michigan had a 20 foot board with yellow flashing border lights and light behind the copy. On wear, the only evidence I found is that the 1962 Columbus Day storm blew the 99W's letters half a mile. No source names a typeface, so "Roman block" is unsupported here.

**Diary mapping (raw Phase 2 idea, not a decision).** A two-slot "tonight" board that shows what the user is watching now and next, changes in one action, and has a fixed character limit.

## 2. In-car speakers and speaker posts

**Real function.** The speaker moved sound from a public nuisance to a private control. Early sites used large speakers at the screen, and the 1933 patent allows units near the screen or spread across the field. RCA tried speakers buried under each car, then designed its first in-car speaker and power supply in 1940, per Radio Age. In the first version patrons received a portable speaker and plugged it into a receptacle by the space. The later unit stayed on a pedestal with a coiled cord drawn through the window. International Projectionist in 1950 calls the in-car speaker the largest single factor in public acceptance, because it removed the noise problem and gave the patron control of the sound. The visit went like this. The patron took the speaker from the post, hung it on the window, set the volume, and put it back before starting the car.

RCA's 1945 patent application shows the engineering. Each post carried a transformer feeding two speakers, so a short circuit in one cable could not silence a whole ramp. Most later patents exist because of what patrons did. Borberg's 1949 application says users "impatiently drop the loud speaker and drive away". Melkon's 1955 application adds a switch in the speaker arm because unused speakers stayed on, wasted current and disturbed neighbours, and ushers had to walk the lot turning them off. The Autocrat patent, filed in 1947, puts a neon lamp on the junction box for each speaker. The lamp lit if the speaker was pulled off or the cord cut, it could also call a car hop, and its brightness told the projectionist about volume. Showmen's Trade Review in 1946 says cars collided with posts, and that the theatre paid for car windows broken by forgotten speakers, with insurance covering the cost. Post lights helped patrons see to remove and replace speakers.

**Visual vocabulary.** The Henry Ford's EPRAD unit is aluminium and wire, in silver colour, 8 by 4.5 by 6.25 inches, with raised vertical "EPRAD" lettering on the front. Radio Age describes a weatherproof speaker with a neoprene-covered coiled cord, about 16 inches retracted and stretching to about nine feet, and a volume control on the case. RCA's patent shows a concrete base, tubular standard, junction box with removable cover, and wire baskets on each side. The boxes could be removed for winter. At the 99W the poles are steel with steel-covered junction boxes labelled RCA, set in concrete cuboid bases, and two cars fit between posts. Theatre Catalog 1954-55 recommends aluminium or grey enamel for speakers and posts. The Henry Ford article calls the speakers grey and reports "something akin to a mutiny" when owners tried to remove the poles.

**Diary mapping (raw Phase 2 idea, not a decision).** Put the one control a user changes during a watch at the entry itself, and ask them to close it out with a single "hang it back" action before they leave.

## 3. Ramps and row layout

**Real function.** A ramp tilted the car so its occupants could see the screen over the cars in front. Hollingshead's patent, granted in 1933, sets arcuate stall-ways around the screen, each inclined upward toward the screen and each slightly below the one behind it, so a sight line clears all the cars ahead. Later embodiments add front wheel risers so each driver can adjust the angle. The patrons did the last step themselves. A Filmack trailer script says "Adjust your car on the ramp to obtain a clear view of the screen". Layout was engineered and bought in. The Spud got sight distance and grading specifications from Ballantyne in Omaha. The Sound Track Book tells owners to plot sight lines on a large elevation drawing, allow at least 9 feet per car, and space junction boxes 18 feet apart and 5 feet back from the top of the ramp, on 40 inch posts.

Ramps also solved a wayfinding problem. Showmen's Trade Review in 1946 says it was desirable to number ramps and posts so people could find their way back to their own cars. The Beverly still has lighted signs at each row end, showing the row number at the top and "Full" at the bottom. Exits were part of the design too. The same 1946 article says hundreds left at once at the break, and that some theatres needed sworn deputies for the traffic.

**Visual vocabulary.** The materials are plain. The 99W has gravel pathways and eight earthen mounds in sloped arcs. The Spud has concentric dirt and gravel drives and graded spaces. The Beverly has rough concrete aggregate in slightly tiered semicircles. The patent gives stalls of about 15 to 16 feet and drive-ways of about 35 feet. At night the lines of post lights marked the ramps, which the Sound Track Book says gave the lot a "picturesque appearance".

**Diary mapping (raw Phase 2 idea, not a decision).** Treat row number and "Full" as a locator, so a user can always get back to their exact place in a series or list and see when a set is complete.

## 4. Ticket booths and roll tickets

**Real function.** The booth was the first contact and the point of financial control. It took money, counted people and proved the count later. Ticket machines were built for audit. A 1916 article on the National Ticket-printing Register says it records each ticket before issuing it, which gives the manager "an absolute check on the business". The Automaticket drive-in system in a 1948 advert is the most specific record I found. The seller issued a ticket perforated lengthwise, and the patron took it or left it under the windshield wiper. At the entrance the ticket lifter tore it and returned the stub to the wiper, unfolded, so staff could compare stub length with the number of occupants. Stubs went into a locked rod box in collection order, so missing serial numbers and resales showed up. Patrons could also be sold to by a roving seller carrying a portable machine with 500 numbered tickets, which helped when traffic backed up to the highway.

Counting cars mattered as well. Automaticket sold a car counter that kept a running total of cars in the theatre, and Berezny sold a locked car-counting meter. By 1965 the Car-Chek system used directional treadles in each lane to record a sale only when a car entered, with a lit display of the charge facing the patron and the count facing the checker. Its stated aim was to reduce "cash leakage". Pricing varied. Some theatres charged per car regardless of passengers, while the 99W in 1953 charged per person, with 60 cents for adults, 20 cents for ages 5 to 12, and free entry through age 4. The cashier also answered the show time question. A 1950 catalogue sells a programme schedule holder to save cashiers that time.

**Visual vocabulary.** Booths were small and sat between lanes. The 66 Drive-In's is five by nine feet in waved glass block with Art Deco and Streamline Moderne styling. The 99W's is wood-framed with shingle siding under an octagonal roof with eight foot clearance, with windows facing both the highway and the lot, and Formica boomerang countertops. The Beverly's is metal and glass, dividing two lanes under a once neon-lit canopy, with "BOX OFFICE" spelled in neon. The Spud's has a shallow bow truss roof, clapboard siding and sliding aluminium windows. Details solved cold weather. A plastic louvred grille for the booth's speaker opening let sound through and kept cold air out. On the ticket itself, one source says machines issued carnival or raffle style tickets, the 1916 register printed two prices in different colours, and the 1965 Model ST issued a "flat, continuous" stub. I found no source that states a roll format for drive-ins.

**Diary mapping (raw Phase 2 idea, not a decision).** Give each watch a numbered stub that records date, title and who was there, in a sequence the user can audit, so a gap in the log is visible.

## 5. Intermission countdown reels and snipes

**Real function.** The countdown told a lot full of cars how long they had, and it moved them to the snack bar and back before the next feature. UCLA's archive defines snipes as "pretty much everything other than the movie itself", including local ads, intermission films and theatre policies. Reels were modular. The Henry Ford describes a clock shell, a basic countdown reel that projectionists spliced with other material into a mix-and-match intermission film. Theatre Catalog 1952 gives real timing. Filmack's clock trailer ran ten minutes, with a voice announcing each minute, the clock hand and a centre numeral updating together, and music changing every minute. National Screen Service's version had a three-minute animated film, then a three-minute clock with gongs at two and one minutes, a title reading "The Next Show Is About To Start", and a flashing clock at 30 seconds. Filmack's 1955 catalogue offered more than 50 subjects, including all-purpose, fully animated and merchant intermission clocks. A 1964 catalogue shows that clock trailers could carry local merchants' ads, and that National Screen Service made custom ones.

**Visual vocabulary.** Sources describe a clock disk, with a numeral in the centre in Filmack's version. Filmack lettered reminders around the corners of the film. In National Screen Service's version a hand wiped away the black part of the clock, and snack items appeared in the four corners. A 1964 trailer opens on a neon snack bar sign. Animated food characters are documented, such as the dancing hot dogs and popcorn the Henry Ford mentions. *Let's All Go to the Lobby* is a 1957 Filmack snipe, and UCLA says it joined the National Film Registry in 2000. The Henry Ford holds the 1964 National Screen Service reel "Refreshment Time" with *The Hungry Martian*. The Internet Archive's "Drive-In Movie Ads" item holds concession ads it dates to the 1950s and early 1960s. Trailer copy uses capitals and ellipses heavily, for example "It's INTERMISSION TIME NOW!".

**Diary mapping (raw Phase 2 idea, not a decision).** A countdown tied to a real next event, such as the next episode's release, that says how long remains and what to do before then.

Sources: [UCLA Film & Television Archive](https://cinema.ucla.edu/blog/pre-show-entertainment-and-beyond); [The Henry Ford, Ford-Wyoming article](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/articles/detail/articles/2013/12/20/ford-wyoming-drive-in-scrapping-at-the-screen); [Theatre Catalog 1952, intermission scripts](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/theatre-catalog-1952_0374); [Theatre Catalog 1952, snack bar scripts](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/theatre-catalog-1952_0375); [Theatre Catalog 1964](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/theatre-catalog-1964_0062); [Motion Picture Herald 1955, Filmack catalogue](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/motionpictureher198quig_0560); [Cinema Treasures, reader comments only, weak](https://www.cinematreasures.org/blog/2007/9/14/intermission-films); [Internet Archive metadata](https://archive.org/metadata/DriveInMovieAds/metadata).

## 6. Concession stand and menu boards

**Real function.** The stand had to serve a whole lot during one short break. Theatre Catalog 1950-51 and 1958 compare two systems. In the station system one attendant prepared, served and took money at a single point, which needed skilled staff and suited smaller theatres. In the cafeteria system patrons walked a line, served themselves, and paid one cashier at the end. The 1958 article calls this "an absolute necessity" where two features run with an intermission, because many patrons leave their cars at once. It also says every item was on view along the line, which raised purchases per person, and it estimates that 95 per cent of new drive-ins in one New England period had cafeteria stands. One Massachusetts site used signs saying "Serve Yourself". The walk itself was a cost. The Exhibitor in 1950 describes patrons walking a quarter of a mile over gravel while balancing drinks and popcorn. The Henry Ford quotes the point that food had to be ready quickly so it could be carried back to the car before the next feature. In 1966 Ballantyne proposed radio receivers in concession areas so patrons could keep listening.

**Visual vocabulary.** The 99W stand has a counter with a steel railing to direct customer traffic, a late 1940s Manley popcorn machine, and original 1950s and 1960s signs from Coca-Cola and others. The Beverly's has an iron railing along the counter and viewing windows onto the screen. The Spud's has a large fixed window with a view of the screen from the concession area. The Caro's counter was knotty pine. Theatre Catalog 1954-55 advises yellow-gold walls and a "loud" palette inside and a flat exterior finish to cut headlight glare. The only menu board evidence I found is one line in the 1958 catalogue, which places the menu on the rear display wall in "generous-size lettering easy-to-read for patrons". It also calls the Manley popper a "silent salesman". At every site I read in detail the stand shared a building with the projection booth and rest rooms. Sources are in section 7.

**Diary mapping (raw Phase 2 idea, not a decision).** One linear pass that shows every option in view and asks the user to commit once at the end, for logging a watch in the short gap between episodes.

Sources: [Theatre Catalog 1958, p. 90](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/theatre-catalog-1958_0090); [Theatre Catalog 1958, p. 91](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/theatre-catalog-1958_0091); [Theatre Catalog 1950-51](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/theatre-catalog-1950_0258); [The Exhibitor 1950](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/exhibitormayjul144jaye_0268); [Theatre Catalog 1966, Ballantyne](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/theatre-cat-1966_0040); [Theatre Catalog 1954-55, colour advice](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/theatare-catalog-1954_0158); [Theatre Catalog 1950-51, the Caro](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/theatre-catalog-1950_0236); [The Henry Ford, Ford-Wyoming article](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/articles/detail/articles/2013/12/20/ford-wyoming-drive-in-scrapping-at-the-screen); 99W, Beverly and Spud nominations, URLs in section 3.

## 7. Projection booth

**Real function.** The booth put an adequate picture on a very large outdoor screen and kept it running without a visible break. Carbon arc light set the working pattern. The Spud nomination says the carbon arc gave only 20 minutes of bright light, so the theatre needed two projectors and trained staff to coordinate film segments. Xenon lamps later removed the segmented film and the operator's cost. Brightness was a constant compromise. Motion Picture Herald in 1955 says drive-ins accepted lower screen brightness than indoor houses, gives about 4 foot-candles as a working figure with arcs at 123 to 135 amperes, and sets wide screen limits of about 100 feet for CinemaScope. Booth position mattered. The Hollingshead patent puts the booth below the angle of vision and blows filtered air through a funnel to keep insects off the lens. British Kinematography in 1953 notes that the projector tilted up to the screen, the reverse of a typical indoor theatre. The booth also ran the sound, and in the Autocrat patent it watched volume indirectly through post lamps. Ramp lighting circuits ran from the projection room too.

**Visual vocabulary.** The booths were utilitarian and usually shared a building with concessions. The 99W's sits on the second floor over the rest rooms, with exterior wood stairs, two Motiograph projectors from 1953, brown hardboard walls, and small lens openings covered by open-ended metal boxes. The Spud's is one end of a low clapboard building with one large and two small openings for the projector. The Beverly's is the two-storey part of a cinder-block building, centred across from the screen. The Caro combined the concession, projection and rest room functions in one building with RCA projection machines.

**Diary mapping (raw Phase 2 idea, not a decision).** Borrow the changeover, so the next episode or next title in a run is already cued before the current one ends and the user never sees the handover.

Sources: [Spud Drive-In nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/7bd978e7-93fe-4009-a6b1-3102c67f3216); [Motion Picture Herald 1955](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/motionpictureher198quig_0324); [US1909537A, Hollingshead](https://patents.google.com/patent/US1909537A/en); [British Kinematography 1953](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/britishk2122brit_0207); [US2463339A, Autocrat](https://patents.google.com/patent/US2463339A/en); [The Sound Track Book of the Theatre, undated](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/soundtrackbookof00unse_0431); [99W Drive-In nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/e726045f-1c08-4521-9f41-485e1094a45f/); [Beverly Drive-In nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/NRHP/GetAsset/NRHP/08000761_text); [Theatre Catalog 1950-51, the Caro](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/theatre-catalog-1950_0236).

## 8. Neon and sign typography

**Real function.** Most of the neon I found was advertising aimed at the road or the entrance. The rules for the viewing side ran the other way. International Projectionist in 1957 says a light-struck screen destroys the picture, that lights on billboards and ticket offices must be shaded from the audience, and that floodlights on the screen tower should be off during shows. Neon was also expensive. Theatre Catalog 1954-55 suggests reflective glass-bead paint on towers and signs specifically to "reduce the need for expensive neon tubing designs and signs". Directional signs had a separate job. Ardley's 1954 signs used yellow letters on dark blue so they could be read by day, and fluorescent letters that reflected headlights by night. Two uses sit inside the lot. The Caro's concession roof was neon-lit, and the Autocrat patent proposes a neon signal lamp in each junction box. I do not know how widely that patent was built.

**Visual vocabulary.** The Valley Drive-In in Ontario, California, lit an 80 foot tower mural with 2,500 feet of neon tubing. The Caro's tower carried rose, green and yellow neon, and its overhanging roof was neon-lit. The Beverly has neon lights on the screen tower, including its logo with a moon and shooting stars, and the current script logo covers about half of the 75 foot width. The 66 Drive-In has an original steel and neon sign beside Route 66. Not every site used neon. The 99W's triangular blade sign uses blinking red and white bulbs. The Spud used a 1946 truck carrying a giant potato in place of a sign. The Park Theatre in Estes Park, an indoor house, shows the same logic in 1926, when an 80 foot tower with neon-accented arch was added as a method of advertising. Evidence on typography is limited to script for the Beverly and "large readable letters" as a stated goal. I found no source that names faces or sign lettering practice.

**Diary mapping (raw Phase 2 idea, not a decision).** Keep brightness for getting people in, on entry points and empty states, and keep the surface where the user reads and watches dark and quiet.

## 9. Screen towers

**Real function.** The tower held a huge picture up against the wind, and its other face advertised to the highway. Theatre Catalog 1953-54 calls the screen a huge "sail in the winds". The Camden screen was wood-framed, the Union, New Jersey, rebuild used steel, and designers moved to I-beam columns, concrete anchors and welded steel plates partly because a labour dispute made wood panels expensive to erect. Storms still won. The 99W lost its first screen in the 1962 Columbus Day Storm and its second in 1991, and it used the wreck of the first to build a maintenance shed. The patent sets the screen near the road and back from it for shielding from outside light, and International Projectionist describes a shadow box for northern twilight. CinemaScope forced physical change, and both the Spud in 1955 and the 66 Drive-In widened their screens.

The back was the billboard. The NPS calls the 66's 66 foot steel screen house a dual role structure, with the screen on the front and a billboard on the sloped back. The Spud's back carries a billboard shaped like an Idaho licence plate. The Corral in Raymondville, Texas, had a cowboy mural visible five miles away. Towers could hold rooms. The Beverly's owners built their family home beneath the main screen, sound and light-proofed, and a later renovation added a faux New Orleans front on the non-viewing side.

**Visual vocabulary.** The Spud's tower is an unenclosed heavy timber frame on concrete pilings, about 35 by 60 feet, built from bought plans. The 99W's screen is corrugated metal on steel I-beams, painted white on the audience side, with steel bracing that also faces the audience. The Beverly's is brick masonry sheathed in white asbestos-cement tiles, about 105 by 75 feet, with paired Tudor arched openings for cars. The Valley's is Robinson decking and stucco. Theatre Catalog 1954-55 gives one painted example of yellow, red and blue stripes over white with glass beads.

**Diary mapping (raw Phase 2 idea, not a decision).** One object with two faces, a working surface for the owner and a public face for visitors, as a way to think about a diary and a shared profile.

## 10. FM tuning signs

**Real function.** Radio sound replaced a wired speaker for every space with a single number the patron tuned to, so the number became the instruction. Other ideas came first. A patent filed in 1955 and 1958 proposed buried induction loops with a pickup in each car, citing patrons who drive away with speakers attached. In 1966 Ballantyne sold the DS-7, a low power AM transmitter in the booth, whose advert describes it as an addition alongside existing speakers and says the signal stays within the theatre's area. The dated local records put the change later. Shankweiler's added AM in 1982 and kept the speakers, and it claims to be the first drive-in with FM stereo in 1986. The 99W added AM in 1983 and FM in 1992. The Spud added AM in 1989 and FM in the early 1990s, and lightning destroyed its AM station in 1997. The 99W nomination says its transmitter reaches a couple of hundred feet and has to go a little past the property to avoid black spots. Today the number is part of the rules, such as 96.5 FM at the 99W and 90.7 FM at Shankweiler's. Shankweiler's warns that phone streaming apps and Bluetooth devices will not work, and that staff jump-start cars whose batteries die with the radio on.

**Visual vocabulary.** I found no source that describes a physical FM tuning sign, so this part is thin. What I can source is what stayed behind. Speakerless poles remain at the 66, the 99W and the Beverly, and at the 99W they now mark parking spaces. The Henry Ford reports near mutiny when owners tried to remove the Ford-Wyoming's poles. Shankweiler's and the 99W state frequencies as a plain figure on their rules pages.

**Diary mapping (raw Phase 2 idea, not a decision).** Reduce setup to the single piece of information a new user needs, and show it at the gate.

## 11. Car-hop trays

**Real function.** The tray belongs to drive-in restaurants, and the car hop belongs to both. Every tray patent I read describes restaurant or curb service. McGinley's Service Tray from Dallas, filed in 1931, hooks a pair of legs between the window opening and the glass and describes curb service. Lee's Car-hop Serving Tray, filed in 1964, names drive-in restaurants, as does the Browns' Drive-in Food Tray, also filed in 1964. None of them mentions theatres. The restaurant origin is weakly sourced here, because the only page I read is a reader's letter in American Heritage, which dates the Pig Stand to 1921 and gives the running board story for the word carhop.

Car hops did work at drive-in theatres, though I found no window tray there. The Exhibitor in 1950 reports a split. Some operators said car hops sold 60 to 70 per cent of their concessions, and others said they annoyed patrons. The hard part was interrupting people during a film. The Autocrat patent aims to end the need for car hops to disturb patrons who do not want service. A theatre version of the service was a call signal. The Sound Track Book describes junction boxes with a red light so a patron can summon an attendant, which "eliminates the need for car solicitation". British Kinematography describes a paging switch that flashes a light in the concession stand. A Filmack script points patrons to a cart marked by small red lights, with an attendant called Oscar passing the ramps. Car hops needed physical access, and the Sound Track Book's spacing advice mentions it. Combined theatre and restaurant sites existed too. Theatre Catalog 1955-56 shows one using a Servus-Fone ordering system that cut a car hop's trips to one per car.

**Visual vocabulary.** McGinley's tray has bent legs, a collapsible brace and a holder that keeps the price check readable and dry. Lee's tray has rubber sleeves on the legs and a cushioned prop. The Browns' patent says fixed carside stands with intercoms and swinging trays were costly. Materials for tray bodies are not stated. The Caro's overhanging neon-lit roof partly sheltered car hops on bad nights.

**Diary mapping (raw Phase 2 idea, not a decision).** Service that comes only when called, so reminders and suggestions stay silent until the user asks for them.

## 12. Rain and sound instructions

**Real function.** Instructions taught a new kind of audience how to behave in a car, and they mostly arrived on the screen. Theatre Catalog 1952 prints "Special Copy for Drive-In Trailers" with an "Instructions" group. The Filmack and National Screen Service scripts cover the whole visit:

- Speaker. "Take Speaker from post and hang it on the inside of your car window." "Turn volume control knob to regulate volume you desire."
- Parking. "Park your car so that you will take up only one space between the speaker posts."
- Lights. "Please keep ALL LIGHTS out during the performance". Patrons should also use the hand brake or leave the car in gear so stop lights do not flash at the row behind.
- Leaving. "before you start your car, or even release the brake, please remember to replace your individual speaker on the post". Also "Drive Forward", "Do not back up" and "Enter exit lane slowly".
- Heaters. Patrons were reminded to replace the heater on the post beside the car.
- Cigarettes. "Don't flip your lighted cigarettes out of your car!"

Compliance was imperfect. International Projectionist in 1957 says headlights still flared despite large signs asking for parking lights only, and adds "(Diplomacy is required of attendants.)"

Rain was a tested condition from the start. Motion Picture Herald in 1933 reports a test during a downpour at Camden, where wipers "offered some aid to vision". Showmen's Trade Review in 1946 says patrons already present "start their motors and windshield wipers and sit it out", that the screen shows through light rain, and that "There are no rain checks, as a rule". American Speech in 1957, as quoted in the 99W nomination, reports "fog checks" issued when sleet or fog hid the screen. Luther in 1951, also quoted there, lists fog filters, glycerin compounds for windshields, and DDT spraying for insects. Theatres also sold or rented rain visors. A Filmack script promises "The show goes on rain or clear", and a "Free Gas" trailer offers fuel on nights cold or wet enough to need heaters or wipers. Modern rules say much the same. Shankweiler's runs "rain or shine" and asks for all exterior lights off. The 99W says "Lights out, please".

**Visual vocabulary.** I found no surviving printed windshield notice, so this part is thin. The windshield held the ticket stub, and instructions were film trailers, signs and later web pages. Rain visors are the physical artefacts. A 1950 version was flexible plastic in colours or transparent, on a metal frame under the doors or stretched across the roof to the hood ornament. Rainview's 1954 visor was heavy plastic on an aluminium tube frame in red, blue, green, yellow or pink, with a suggested rental of 20 cents. The 1961 Drizzle Gard was collapsible, sold at the stand, and backed by a film trailer and free poster signs and counter racks.

**Diary mapping (raw Phase 2 idea, not a decision).** Show each instruction once, on the surface the user is already looking at, at the moment it applies.

## Misconceptions

Each item below is a claim the sources contradict, or a negative finding, and I say which. Sources are in the sections named or in the list at the end.

- **Arcade.** Negative finding. No artefact, nomination, patent or trailer script I read connects arcades or arcade buttons to drive-ins. The Spud nomination's list of amenities names play areas, picnic areas, miniature golf, driving ranges, and live music and dancing, and it has no arcade. The nearest thing is a 2007 Cinema Treasures comment about a 1960s game room at the Eastlake, which is anecdote. Arcades do have a film link, but it is indoor and earlier. *The House That Shadows Built* places a Penny Arcade on Fourteenth Street where cinema ran in "little boxes" before a five cent picture show opened upstairs, and it names Adolph Zukor among the owners of the Penny Arcade opened in the 1903 to 1904 season. The control a patron actually touched was a volume knob. My Lantern search for "penny arcade" returned no drive-in results, but that search was shallow, so a game room in some site's concession building remains possible.
- **VHS.** Sourced. Video cassette recorders appear in the sources as a cause of decline. The Spud nomination lists cable television, video cassette recorders and home entertainment centres together as causes, and the 99W nomination repeats this. VHS belongs to the end of the story, and nothing I read treats it as part of the drive-in experience.
- **Film strips.** Negative finding. 35mm film was real, but it stayed in the booth, where projectionists spliced reels, per the Henry Ford article in section 5. No sign, marquee, ticket or menu description I read uses a film strip motif. Patrons saw the projected picture and never handled film.
- **Neon everywhere.** Contradicted, see section 8. Most neon I found faced the road or the entrance, with the Caro's concession roof as an exception. Lot lighting had to be shaded and tower floods turned off during shows. Some sites had bulbs or a potato truck in its place, and one catalogue recommends paint as a cheaper alternative.
- **The car hop tray on the window.** Partly wrong for theatres. The tray patents I read describe restaurants. Theatres had car hops, carts and call lights, and no source I read shows a window tray at a theatre.
- **Speakers as the permanent drive-in sound.** Contradicted. Early sound came from speakers near the screen, and at sites I checked radio arrived in the 1980s. Poles often outlived the speakers.
- **In-car speaker dates.** The sources differ. Radio Age says RCA designed its first system in 1940, and International Projectionist says RCA introduced it in 1941. The Spud nomination credits RCA and places the fix in the postwar account. Treat the speaker as a 1940s development.
- **Hollingshead's patent and rain.** Negative finding. The patent does not mention rain or wipers. The rain evidence I found comes from the 1933 trade report.
- **First-run glamour.** Contradicted. The Spud nomination says drive-ins mostly showed B-grade or second-run films, and it describes the audience as families, with children in footed pyjamas. The 99W nomination also records "passion pit" as slang, so both uses are real.
- **Theming as pure decoration.** Partly wrong. The Corral's chuck wagon snack bar and cowboy mural, the Valley's Spanish mural and the Spud's potato were real and themed. They had a job, which was to catch drivers on the highway. The 99W nomination says the same of its sign form.
